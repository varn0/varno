There is a popular sentiment that everyone in a tech org—from the PM to the Site Reliability Engineer—needs a deep, immersive understanding of the business domain. Sometimes it comes in the form of "dogfooding," where everyone is required to use the company's products.

I was actually wondering whether to write about this or not. But just today, there was a lengthy tweet from Karpathy, a Martin Fowler post, and three Hacker News articles I wanted to read all at once.

Cognitive load is real.

The amount of information people have to handle nowadays is overwhelming. If we demand that our DevOps experts become subject matter experts in an underlying industry, we dilute their focus on what actually matters: The Software Development Life Cycle.

While Product Owners and Backend Architects must live and breathe the industry (Fintech, Healthtech, E-commerce) to build the right logic, the DevOps team operates in a different domain entirely: The Domain of Delivery and DevEx.

For a DevOps Engineer, the "Business" is the platform. Their "Customers" are the developers.

I am not saying DevOps should be complete strangers to the business domain, or avoid "dogfooding" on a regular basis. I am saying there should be a knowledge gradient. In many cases, it's okay if a DevOps engineer has little to no knowledge of the underlying business domain.

- **Product/Architects:** Need deep immersion to translate business rules into code.
- **DevOps Leads:** Need Strategic Awareness. Especially in smaller companies where the DevOps Lead is also the Cloud Architect. They need to know business cycles and processes to anticipate infrastructure scaling and budget/resource allocation.
- **DevOps Engineers:** Need Functional Awareness. They need to know the technical requirements, priorities, and sometimes the "Why" behind a specific business pivot to stay motivated.

A DevOps team that spends too much time in the "Business Domain" risks losing focus. They might waste time wondering why a product isn't gaining traction when their focus should be on shortening the delivery lifecycle to increase user feedback—letting others determine if the product is on the right track.

Finally, picture this: In the face of a new release, do you want your DevOps team wondering why this or that feature is going forward, or just thinking about how to get it out there faster? It makes sense to be a generalist in a very small team, but in a larger organization, I've seen over-immersion lower motivation and slow down the gears.

The takeaway? Let your DevOps team master the business of building software. That is the domain where they provide the highest ROI.
